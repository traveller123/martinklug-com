For years I surrounded myself with people who agreed with me.

Not deliberately. It happens by drift. You keep going back to the conversations that are easy, and after a while everyone close to you has broadly the same view of what you are building and how it is going.

It felt like support. What it actually did was add about a year to every correction.

The people who have been most useful to me are the ones who said something was not working while there was still time to do something about it. One of them told me to stop putting money into a business I was attached to. I ignored him for eight months. Those eight months cost considerably more than any advice he could have charged me for.

## Nobody tells you when it is good enough

The problem gets worse when you work for yourself. In a company there is always someone above the work. A manager, a committee, a client who signs off. You calibrate against them. Good enough gets defined by somebody else, and there is real relief in that even when you resent it.

That disappears when you own the thing. Some days the bar goes too high. I have spent a week on something that needed a morning, because there was nobody to tell me it was finished. Other days it drops without me noticing and I get away with work I would have sent back if a colleague had produced it. Both are the same problem: no outside calibration.

What has helped is not discipline (I have a normal amount, which is to say not much on a bad week). It is having two or three people who look at what I am doing and say the honest thing. Not encouragement. Assessment. One of them tells me when I am polishing something nobody asked for, and that has saved me more time than any system I have tried.

## The best thing my co-founder did

The best thing my co-founder did was contradict me in public.

Co-founder conflict gets discussed as a personality problem. Mine mostly was not. We argued about the business constantly and almost never about who was right. There is a version of a disagreement where two people are trying to find the answer, and a version where two people are defending a position they took a month ago and would now quite like to keep.

We split what we each owned early, in writing, before there was anything worth arguing about. Not out of distrust. Because the moment there is actual money on the table, "we'll figure it out later" becomes a negotiation with someone you used to like.

And the one I underrated at the time: he was comfortable telling me I was wrong in front of other people. It felt bad every single time. It was worth more than most of the advice I have paid for since.

## Who I now want in the room

Competence is the easiest thing to check about a business partner, and the least predictive. Everyone I have seriously considered building something with was capable. That is the entry ticket, not the decision. Three other things decide it.

Can they commit fully, and will they. Someone can be entirely willing and have a life that does not currently allow it. I have watched good partnerships come apart on this alone, with nobody at fault and everybody disappointed. Enthusiasm is not availability.

Agency. Do they move without being asked. Something breaks over a weekend and they either deal with it or they write it up for the next meeting. I do not believe this can be installed in an adult, and I have stopped trying.

Personal sympathy. Do I want to spend several years and a large number of difficult conversations with this person. It sounds like the soft one and I would now weight it highest. The partnerships that worked for me were with people I still liked when things were bad, and the only place you learn that is in the bad period, which is late.

I have not fully solved any of this. I still feel the pull towards the easy conversation, particularly when things are going badly and I would like one hour of not thinking about it. The change is smaller than it sounds. I stopped reading agreement as loyalty.
