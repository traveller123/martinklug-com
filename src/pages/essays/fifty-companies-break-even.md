Fifty-something companies later, I am roughly at break-even.

I expect it to end up as an ok profit. Which, said plainly, means that for the money and the years involved there are other asset classes with better returns, lower risk and considerably less work. Anyone telling you angel investing is a clever way to grow capital is selling something, or has had one good exit and stopped counting.

That is the financial column. The rest of the ledger is why I am still doing it.

Euphoria. Backing something when it was two people and a document, and watching it turn into a company with real customers and real employees.

Friendship. Several of the people I invested in are now the people I call when I have a decision I cannot see clearly myself.

Disappointment. Founders who went silent. Companies that died of something avoidable while I watched from a distance and had no standing to stop it.

Anger. Money that went on things it was not raised for.

And once, straightforward fraud. That one took a long time to get over, less because of the money and more because I had liked them.

The return I have actually had sits in the other columns. What I learned. Who I met. The pattern recognition I could not have bought at any price, which now makes me a better operator in my own businesses.

## How I choose, after getting it wrong both ways

My first investments were basically vibes. I backed people I liked. Someone would explain their idea, I would feel something in the room, and I would sign up. A few of those worked. Most did not, and the ones that failed mostly failed for reasons that were clear from the numbers the whole time, if I had bothered to look.

So I corrected. For a while I only looked at the model. Margin, retention, cash conversion, how much of the growth was being paid for. If the spreadsheet did not hold up, I passed, whoever was sitting across the table.

That was also wrong, just slower and more respectable. I passed on people I should have backed. Early-stage numbers are mostly assumptions with a nice font applied to them. Judging an eighteen-month-old company on its model is largely judging the founder's Excel.

Where I am now: both, with a slight bias towards the numbers. The numbers tell me whether the thing can work at all. The founder tells me what happens when the plan meets reality, which is never the plan in the deck. If I only get one of the two, I take the founder.

Most of my passes have nothing to do with the market. Market size and competition are the objections founders prepare for, and they usually prepare well. What decides it is smaller. How someone talks about the last three months, not the next three years. Whether the numbers in the deck match the numbers in the conversation. What happens when I say something inaccurate about their own business (the good ones correct me straight away, without apologising for it).

And the one that ends conversations fastest: what has changed their mind recently. If nothing has moved in a year, either nothing has been properly tested, or something was tested and the result got filed somewhere polite.

## What an angel actually changes

You can make angel investing a full-time job. It changes almost nothing.

The available activity has no ceiling. Decks to review. Events. Founder coffees. Calls with other investors about deals neither of you will do. Five days a week can be filled with it, and every hour of it looks like work.

The admission underneath is that an angel's influence is very limited. You hold a small part of the cap table. You are not on the board. You do not see the numbers monthly. The founder makes the call in the morning and you hear about it in a quarterly update, if there is one.

So the question I now put to my own calendar is which of this changes an outcome. The money, which is done at the moment of transfer. An introduction to a specific person who buys, hires or invests. Twenty honest minutes before they do something expensive. Being reachable when it is going badly.

That list is short and most of it takes very little time. Everything else is participation. It feels like being an investor, and it is a hobby with a lot of calendar invitations attached.

If the point is compounding capital, there are easier ways. I have never found a better way to stay close to how things actually get built. Angel investing has been the least efficient financial decision I have made and one of the best decisions overall. Those are two separate ledgers, and it helps to keep them apart.
