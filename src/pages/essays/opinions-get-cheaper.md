I give much better advice about companies I do not own.

As an investor I can look at a business, see the obvious thing, say it plainly, and be right more often than the founder is. Not because I am smarter. Because I am not the one who has to carry it out, and if I am wrong it costs me a cheque.

When it was my own company, that same clarity was never available to me. I usually knew what the right call probably was. I also knew who I would have to tell, what it would do to the person who had built the thing I was about to cut, and what my own situation at home looked like if the number did not recover in time.

That knowledge does not make you smarter. It makes you slower.

My judgement in those years was carrying my mortgage, my pride, and my read of what the team thought of me. I made decisions in that weather for a long time, and some of them were worse because of it. Sitting on the other side of the table, I get the interesting part of the problem and none of the weather.

I used to treat the slowness as a weakness in myself. I now think it is the difference between an opinion and a decision. An opinion costs nothing. A decision has your name on it and usually somebody else's livelihood inside it.

## What the distance is good for

A founder describes something that has them stuck, and often I have been stuck in exactly that shape. Not a similar shape. The same one. I can say what I did, what it cost, and how long it took me to admit it was not working. That takes forty minutes and can save someone a year.

There is self-interest in it too, so I may as well say it. I like being around people who are early and slightly unreasonable. I learn more per hour in those conversations than almost anywhere else, and after a hundred of them you start to see the patterns that no single company ever shows you.

Some of those companies work. Plenty do not. The ones that do rarely look like the ones I expected.

## What the distance is not

Two things follow, and I try to hold both at once.

I say what I see, directly, because a founder gets enough hedged politeness from everywhere else. Being the person willing to say the uncomfortable thing is most of what a small cheque buys you the right to do.

And I stay aware that what I am handing over is an opinion, formed at a distance, without the constraints they can see and I cannot. The person who has to sign it gets the last word, and should.

The founders I have learned most from are the ones who took part of the advice, ignored the rest, and told me afterwards why. That is a specific kind of confidence and it is rarer than it should be.

Opinions get cheaper the further you sit from the consequences. It is worth knowing which one you are being handed, and which one you are handing out.
