The three most expensive mistakes I made as a founder had nothing dramatic about them. In each case I was watching a number. It was just not the one that decided anything.

## The month we almost ran out of money, the P&L looked fine

That was 2012. Revenue on the books, every internal report saying the business was working. Then payments came around and the account did not have enough in it.

The gap was not performance. It was timing. Money we had earned was sitting somewhere else. Money we owed had already left. Nobody in the company was doing anything wrong.

I had spent years reading management accounts (and thinking that made me financially literate). What I could not read was a cash flow forecast that went out further than the end of the quarter.

We got through it. Some of it was a conversation with suppliers we put off for too long. Some of it was luck.

What stayed with me is how ordinary the warning looked. No bad month. No lost client. The numbers that end up in a board pack move slowly. The one that ends you moves daily. Every company I have been involved with since has had a thirteen-week view of cash on a single page. It is the least impressive document in the business and the only one I have never stopped reading.

## I took the cheaper option and paid for it twice

We needed help with sales. Two candidates on the table. One was expensive and clearly good. The other was affordable and probably fine.

I took affordable. I told myself it was disciplined. We were watching every euro at the time and the gap between the two was real money.

Six months later we were roughly where we started, with less runway and a team that had lost a bit of belief in the plan. Then we hired the expensive one anyway. So the saving cost us the first fee, plus six months, plus the credibility I spent asking people to get behind the same thing a second time.

The budget was never really the point. I was managing a number I could defend in a meeting, rather than an outcome nobody was going to ask me about for another two quarters. Fifteen years in large companies had made me very good at justifying the cheaper choice, and no budget I have ever seen has a line for "this will probably not work".

## The money followed the plan. My attention did not.

We opened in Germany a year too early. That part is an ordinary mistake. Costs went up before revenue did. Everybody has a version of it.

The part I actually got wrong was that I stayed in the Netherlands. We started the business there, and I built the structure there: processes, team, the whole operating side. It worked, partly because I was in it every day. Germany needed someone senior on the ground. That someone was me. I knew it at the time. I did not go.

The reasons sounded reasonable, which is how these things survive. The Dutch structure was not finished. I was the one who had built it, so handing it over felt premature. There was always one more thing to tidy up first. All true. Also convenient.

I was spending my attention on the part of the business I already knew how to run, while the expensive new part ran without me. We were paying for a market we had not proved, and the person best placed to prove it was three hours away improving something that already worked fine.

## What the three have in common

The P&L, the hiring budget and the expansion plan were all numbers I could explain to anyone who asked. Cash timing, the cost of six lost months, and where I spent my own days were not on any report, and they decided the outcome each time.

Expansion is an attention decision before it is a budget decision. A profitable month and a solvent month are two different things. And cheap is easy to defend, while the six months are impossible to get back.
