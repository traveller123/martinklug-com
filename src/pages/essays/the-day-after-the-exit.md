There is the launch story. The pivot story. The exit story. Those moments exist. They are also a very small part of the actual experience of building a company.

## The middle

The longest phase of building a company has none of those stories. It is the middle. The part where you show up every day, make decisions with incomplete information, and get almost no signal back.

In corporate, I had quarterly reviews. Clear KPIs. A manager who told me where I stood. As a founder, months can pass where the only feedback is silence. No complaints from customers could mean the product is solid. Or it could mean nobody cares enough to complain. You cannot tell.

Most weeks, nothing dramatic happens. You send the emails, take the calls, update the spreadsheet, follow up with the lead who went silent. At the end of the week you cannot point to a single thing that changed anything in an obvious way. Some weeks the honest status update is: still here, still building.

That used to bother me. It felt like failure, or at least like falling behind. It took me a while to understand that consistency in the absence of drama is most of the job, and that the unremarkable weeks are what actually build a business.

I spent more time in that phase than any other, and I almost never talk about it, because there is nothing dramatic to tell. It is also where most people stop. Not because they fail. Because they run out of patience with the absence of signal.

## The end

The day after the exit was one of the flattest days I can remember.

I had pictured that period for years. Some combination of relief, a good dinner, and the feeling of having finished something. What actually happened was signing, then administration, then a long run of emails, and then an ordinary morning with nothing in the diary.

The money arrives and does the thing money does, which is less than you expect and mostly in the background. The part I had not prepared for was the silence.

For years the company had answered a question I did not know I was asking. What am I for today. It answered it every morning, whether I wanted it to or not. Problems arrived, and my job was to be the person who dealt with them. Then it stopped, and the question was still there, without the thing that had been quietly answering it.

I am aware this is a good problem. It is still a real one, and almost nobody talks about it honestly, because the socially acceptable version of an exit ends at the wire transfer.

Most founders I know went through some version of it. The ones who handled it best had something they were moving towards, not just something they were selling. An exit answers the financial question and opens a different one. It is worth thinking about that second question before the first one is settled.
